### Summary

# Summary of Dhammarato's Dhamma Talk (October 11)

## Core Theme: "Train, Don't Cultivate"

Dhammarato's central message is a distinction between **cultivation** and **training** in Dharma practice:

- **Cultivation** is passive—like someone who goes to the gym to socialize but never actually exercises, or a football team owner who recruits fans rather than training players.
- **Training** is active, repetitive effort—like athletes practicing skills over and over until they become strong and proficient.

He notes that the Anapanasati Sutta uses the language of "training oneself," not cultivating. The goal is to **train in Sukkha** (satisfaction/happiness), not merely cultivate it.

## Key Simile: The Young Tree and the Cows

Drawing from a Goenka story, Dhammarato explains:
- A young tree needs a **fence** to protect it from roaming cows that would eat its leaves.
- Once grown tall and strong, the tree's leaves are out of reach—it becomes a refuge for many beings.
- Similarly, practitioners need protection (through training) until the mind becomes strong enough to withstand the "cows" of the world.

He calls these cows **"GREB"**—Governments, Religion, Education, Business (media, advertising), plus "F" for Fools. These forces will "eat you alive" if you don't carry your practice with you.

## The Three Ps

He references Transactional Analysis concepts:
1. **Permission** — to begin practice
2. **Protection** — building the fence through repetitive training
3. **Potency/Power** — the strength that comes from sustained practice

## Practical Training Method

Rather than passive observation (which he critiques in some Vipassana approaches), practitioners should:
- Actively replace unwholesome thoughts with wholesome ones
- Do this **repetitively**—"one wholesome thought after another after another"
- When thoughts like "this isn't good enough" arise, see them clearly and return to gladdening the mind
- Build momentum until joy becomes natural and strong

## Sukkha vs. Dukkha

- **Dukkha** = dissatisfaction, disappointment
- **Sukkha** = satisfaction, comfort, freedom from fear
- By removing hindrances, joy naturally arises—then we train in that joy until it becomes Piti (rapture) and settles into stable Sukkha

He emphasizes that **first Jhana** is sufficient for freedom—higher Jhanas, while possible, aren't necessary and take enormous time.

## Protecting the Mind

Practical advice includes:
- Don't consume news media (it's designed to keep you weak and fearful)
- Guard your senses—don't read billboards, don't engage with advertising
- Spend time with wholesome friends and in wholesome activities
- Don't "test yourself" against unwholesome influences prematurely
- Be pleasant with others but "don't buy what they're selling"

## Final Points

- The "secret" is simply: **keep practicing**
- No magic, no hidden teachings—just repetitive training
- Every wholesome thought is a moment of freedom
- The path is joyful and results come relatively quickly
- A strong mind cannot be deceived or manipulated

The talk concludes with community announcements about Dhammarato's extensive video archive (2,100+ YouTube videos), transcribed talks, books, and the Open Sangha Foundation website.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the key metaphors and stories used, along with their meanings:

### 1. The Young Tree and the Cows (Story from Goenka)
-   **The Story:** A young tree is just beginning to grow leaves. In India, roaming cows are likely to eat all the leaves off of it. To help the tree survive, you must build a fence around it to protect it. Once protected, it can grow into a large, strong tree that supports birds, squirrels, and insects. If a cow eats it while it's young, none of that can happen.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **The young tree** represents a new Dharma student or a mind beginning to practice.
    -   **The cows** represent the "GREB" (Governments, Religion, Education, Business) and fools (add an F) — the negative influences of the world that will "eat you alive" or strip away your practice.
    -   **The fence** represents the training and protection (like guarding the senses) that a practitioner must build to protect a young, vulnerable mind.

### 2. The Football Team: Training vs. Cultivation
-   **The Story/Metaphor:** A football team owner wants to cultivate fans (build an audience), but to make the players perform well, they must **train**. You wouldn't recruit a random person off the street to play; you need someone who has developed skills through repetition and practice.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Cultivation** is like a person who goes to the gym every day just to talk to people and hang out, but never actually works out. This represents people who "love the Dharma" and talk about it but don't actually practice.
    -   **Training** is the repetitive, effortful practice of the football player or weightlifter who actually does the work to build skill and strength. The Buddha's suttas talk about *training* the mind, not just cultivating it.

### 3. The Weightlifter / The Gym
-   **The Story/Metaphor:** A weightlifter doesn't go to the gym to stand around, drink Gatorade, and joke with friends—that's just cultivation. They go to the gym to **train**, doing reps over and over to build muscle. Similarly, a martial artist or musician practices by repeating the same actions endlessly.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Training the mind** is like lifting weights. You start with a small dumbbell (e.g., 20 reps of wholesome thoughts). It's hard at first, but with repetition (100, then 120 reps), it becomes easy and light. The effort produces energy.
    -   **The repetition of wholesome thoughts** (one happy thought after another, 400-500 times) is the "work" that builds a strong mind, just like repeated reps build muscle.

### 4. The Ocean and the Continental Shelf
-   **The Metaphor:** An ocean has the same salty taste everywhere, regardless of how many different rivers (with different colors and flavors) flow into it. When you first enter the Dharma, it's like stepping into the shallow water to learn to swim. The "real" Dharma is off the continental shelf, where the water goes deep.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **The ocean** represents the Dhamma. It tastes the same everywhere (consistency).
    -   **The shallows** represent a beginner's practice.
    -   **The deep ocean (off the continental shelf)** represents the profound reality that requires a strong mind to handle.
    -   **Jumping in the deep part** (vs. just laying down to drink from a stream) represents the full commitment of *training*, which is what a "stream enterer" does.

### 5. The Tether of the Farm Animal
-   **The Metaphor:** A cow or donkey is physically tethered to a plow or a millstone and must walk in circles. Humans, however, are generally not physically bound in this way.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **The mental tether** is the unwholesome thought or the "drudgery" of going to work when you don't want to. We are not physically forced, but we are mentally bound by our own minds. By training the mind, we remove these mental fetters.

### 6. Angels and Lightness
-   **The Metaphor (a common saying):** "The reason that angels can fly is because they take themselves so lightly."
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Lightness** comes from training the mind to be joyful and free. As you practice being "light," you actually become light.

### 7. Falling in Love
-   **The Metaphor:** The operative word in "falling in love" is **"fall."** When you are strong, you don't fall down.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Falling in love** represents neediness, weakness, and pining for things. It chases people away and attracts the "GREB" (negative influences). Being strong and happy all the time is attractive, and you don't need to "love yourself" because you nurture and care for yourself through training.

### 8. The "Aha! I See That!" Practice
-   **The Story:** When a practitioner has a wholesome thought but then an unwholesome thought follows ("Yeah, but..."), they are not gladdening the mind. The unwholesome thought is "destroying the training."
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Catching the thought** is like a game. When an unwholesome thought arises, you say, **"Aha! I see that!"** and then joyfully come back to the wholesome. This turns the unwholesome thought into a trigger for a new wholesome thought, which is the repetition that builds strength.

### 9. The Newspaper (If it Bleeds, it Leads)
-   **The Metaphor:** The news media operates on the principle of "if it bleeds, it leads." They focus on horror stories because that's what people are looking for. There have been "happy newspapers," but they all go out of business.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Reading the news** is a form of letting the "cows eat your leaves." It feeds you junk ideas (like junk food) and makes you weak. Since you already have your own happiness, there's no reason to read a "happy newspaper."

### 10. The Potted Plant and the Stream
-   **The Story:** To cultivate is to plant a potted plant, but you must go beyond that to protect it.
-   **The Metaphor:**
    -   **Drinking from a stream:** A person who wants to drink water lays down on the ground and drinks from the stream. This is **cultivation**.
    -   **Jumping in the deep part:** This is **training**. You must go beyond just tasting the water to fully immersing yourself in the practice.

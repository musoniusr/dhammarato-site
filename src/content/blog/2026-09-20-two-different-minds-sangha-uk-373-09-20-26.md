### Summary

# Summary of Dhammarato's Dhamma Talk (September 20th)

## Main Theme: The Fast Mind, the Slow Mind, and the Illusion of Self

This talk continues from the previous day's discussion about the speed of the mind, exploring how modern research distinguishes between two types of mental processing and how this relates to the Buddha's teaching on *anatta* (not-self).

---

## Key Points

### Two Minds: Fast and Slow

**The Fast Mind (Instinctual):**
- Operates on reaction time measured in milliseconds (ordinary humans: 300-350ms; trained martial artists: ~200ms; cats: ~65ms)
- Built into our DNA for self-preservation
- Examples: catching a falling cup, dodging danger, hand gestures, accents
- This is what neurologists might call the "reptilian brain" or what Arya Byrne called the "child state"

**The Slow Mind (Story-Making):**
- Takes half a second to several seconds to process
- Creates narratives about experience
- This is where the "self" arises—the storyteller who tells the story about what happened

### The Two Kinds of Witness

Dhammarato uses the courtroom analogy:
1. **The witness who was there** — direct observation
2. **The witness on the stand** — telling a story about what they remember

Modern science has shown eyewitness testimony is unreliable because memory is *remanufactured* each time we recall it, not simply retrieved. The story changes with each telling.

### How the Self Arises

The self is not permanent or always present. It arises:
- **After** an experience has been processed
- **When** we don't like something (dissatisfaction)
- **Through** the story we tell about what happened
- As a **learned habit** (like a child learning "my toy broke" from a parent's reaction)

Examples given:
- When coding, there's no self—until the code fails ("Oh *me*, it didn't work")
- When exercising, there's no self—until pain arises and we want to quit or push harder
- When watching a movie, there's no self—until the plot doesn't go the way we want

### Anatta: Not "No-Self" but "Not-Self" / Temporary

Dhammarato clarifies common mistranslations:
- **Eternalism**: Believing in a permanent, unchanging soul
- **Nihilism**: Believing there is no self at all
- **Annihilationism**: Believing the self is destroyed at death

The Buddha taught none of these. Instead, *anatta* means everything is **temporary**—including the self. Dhammarato suggests "temporarily-ism" as a better understanding.

### Jati: "Arrival," Not "Rebirth"

A key mistranslation:
- *Jati* does not mean "rebirth" in the metaphysical sense
- It means "arrival" or "here it is"—like a package being delivered
- The "birth" of the self occurs when clinging happens—when we say "I need this" or "this shouldn't be happening"

### The Three Characteristics Working Together

- **Anicca** (impermanence): Something changes
- **Dukkha** (suffering): We don't like the change
- **Anatta** (not-self): The self arises in response

When something changes and there's no self to resist it, there's no dukkha.

### Practical Application: Catching the Self

The practice involves noticing the self arise and letting it go:
- **Beginners**: "Laptop broke... mine... no, not mine" (back-and-forth dialogue)
- **Intermediate**: "Laptop broke... not mine"
- **Advanced**: "Broken laptop... yeah" (catching it before self arises)
- **Mastery**: Seeing things as just "there they are" without storytelling

### Ajahn Chah's Famous Teaching

When asked about beautiful women at a ceremony: "I like it, but I don't want it"—showing the mind catching the process at the *tanha* (craving) stage before *upadana* (clinging).

### Grief and Emotions

- Grief is not necessary—it's a learned behavior
- The vet doesn't grieve over the sick dog; they just do their job
- "Don't get all worked up about it"—being "worked up" is when the self enters
- We can unlearn these habits

### Practical Tools

- **Anapanasati**: When having unwholesome thoughts, it's always "me and my thoughts"—but wholesome thoughts have no self
- **Body awareness**: Tension in the body signals negative thoughts; relaxing the body is having wholesome thoughts
- **Sati** (mindfulness) gets faster with practice, catching the process earlier and earlier

## The Essence

The self is not something permanent that needs to be destroyed. It's a temporary arrival—a habit of storytelling that arises when we resist change. By practicing mindfulness, we learn to catch this process earlier and earlier, eventually seeing experience without adding the story of "me" and "mine." This is liberating because what seemed like a heavy burden turns out to be just a bad habit we can release.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk provided, here are the key metaphors and stories used to explain the concepts of the fast and slow minds, the self, and the nature of experience.

### Metaphors

1.  **The Fast and Slow Minds:** The core metaphor of the talk is the division of the mind into two systems: a "fast mind" (instinctual, reactive, built-in) and a "slower mind" (the storyteller, the part that processes and creates a narrative).

2.  **The Reptilian Brain / The Child State:** The fast mind is metaphorically linked to the "reptilian brain" (a primitive, instinctual part of the brain) and to a "child state," suggesting it is foundational and pre-rational.

3.  **The Witness (Two Kinds):** The speaker uses the metaphor of a witness in two senses:
    - The "witness who was there" (the fast, direct experience).
    - The "witness on the stand" (the slow mind, telling a story about what it remembers).

4.  **The Storyteller and the Story:** The "self" is metaphorically described as the storyteller who creates a narrative about an experience. The story itself is the processed, slower perception of an event. The "self" is not the experience, but the one who claims ownership of the story ("my story").

5.  **The Leaning Tree:** This metaphor explains the relationship between feeling (*Vedana*), craving, and the self. A feeling is like a tree that has been cut and is beginning to **lean**. The direction it leans determines how it will fall. The "crash" of the tree falling is the full-blown emotion and the arising of the self. Wisdom is catching the tree when it is just beginning to lean, before it crashes down.

6.  **"Getting Worked Up":** The speaker uses the common phrase "getting all worked up" as a metaphor. The initial feeling (*Vedana*) is just a "niggle," but "getting worked up" is the later stage where the self arises and creates a full-blown emotion.

7.  **The Package Delivery:** To explain the Pali word *jati* (often translated as "birth"), the speaker uses the metaphor of a package delivery.
    - *Bhava* (development/becoming) is the package in transit.
    - *Jati* (arrival/birth) is the moment the package is handed to you. It has "arrived." This is used to argue that *jati* means the "arrival" of the self, not a literal rebirth.

8.  **The Mechanic and the Vet:** To illustrate how to deal with problems without the self arising, the speaker uses two metaphors:
    - A **mechanic** fixing a broken car doesn't feel bad about the car; he just does his job.
    - A **vet** treating a sick dog is calm and professional, while the owner is crying and in grief. The vet is the metaphor for a wise mind that sees the problem clearly without the added emotional story.

9.  **"You Made That Shit Up":** This blunt phrase is used as a metaphor for the storytelling process. The mind literally fabricates a narrative, and the "self" is the one who creates and identifies with this fabrication.

### Stories and Anecdotes

1.  **The Cat and the Snake:** Used to illustrate the speed of the instinctual "fast mind." A cat's reaction time (65 milliseconds) is far superior to a human's, allowing it to react to a snake almost instantly. This is presented as an example of a built-in, self-preservation instinct.

2.  **The Car Crash and the Two-Tone Car:** To show the unreliability of eyewitness testimony (the "slow mind's" story). The story is told of a car that was red on one side and blue on the other. Two witnesses argued over its color, and the case was thrown out of court. This demonstrates how the "story" made up by the slow mind can be flawed and incomplete.

3.  **Sherlock Holmes and Dr. Watson:** An analogy for investigation versus speculation. Sherlock Holmes represents the investigative mind that gathers facts (like modern science), while Dr. Watson represents the "slow mind" that speculates and creates stories based on limited information.

4.  **The Goenka Retreat Vipassana Story:** A personal anecdote about a meditation retreat where "noble silence" is practiced. The speaker explains how a meditator who has a profound experience will inevitably ruin it by telling someone else about it. The telling of the story ("it was so spectacular!") replaces the actual memory of the experience. This illustrates how the "story" overwrites the "event."

5.  **The First Jhana Story:** A story about a meditator who successfully enters the first *Jhana* (a deep state of concentration) by following instructions. However, after achieving it once, they then try to *re-create* that past experience, which becomes a hindrance and prevents them from succeeding again. This shows how past stories interfere with present reality.

6.  **The Stubbed Toe and the Tender Infant:** To illustrate the delay of the self, the speaker asks how long it takes from stubbing your toe to saying "ouch." He then tells a story from the Buddha about an infant who pulls its hand from a hot coal long before it starts to cry. The physical reaction is fast, but the emotional reaction and the "self" who complains are slow to arrive.

7.  **Donald Trump's Hand Gestures:** An anecdotal observation used to argue that the body often acts on its own, without "our" conscious control. The speaker claims Trump gestures a certain way when he's lying, suggesting the body's "fast mind" is acting without the "self" being aware of it.

8.  **The Old Lady at the Funeral:** A story to explain that grief is often a selfish emotion. The old lady crying at her husband's casket is not thinking about him, but about what *she* has lost. This is used as an example of the self arising in response to an unpleasant feeling.

9.  **The Broken Toy Truck:** A story about a 3-year-old child playing. While the child is absorbed in play, there is "no self." But when the toy breaks, the child's reaction (crying, "my truck!") is the "birth of the self," an identification with the object that was learned (the speaker suggests) from the mother's reaction.

10. **Ajahn Chah and the Beautiful Women:** A story about the Thai forest master Ajahn Chah asking his monk, Ajahn Sumedho, what he thought of some beautifully dressed young women. Sumedho's skilled response, "I like it, but I don't want it," demonstrates a mind that has slowed down the process enough to feel the initial liking (*Vedana*) but has stopped it before it turned into craving and the full-blown self. The speaker notes that for a more advanced practitioner, the reaction would be even faster, seeing them as just "there they are," without even the "liking" story arising.

11. **The Stored Journal:** To illustrate how the story changes over time, the speaker suggests reading an old journal. The intense feelings of the past ("I was brokenhearted about that dog") are now just a story, and the "self" who wrote it is a different self from the one reading it now.
